Sexual addiction, far from being a simple excess of desire, questions the ways in which pleasure, attachment and the body are mobilised. It can be a source of enjoyment, but becomes a symptom when it imposes itself as the only available mode of expression or psychic regulation, at the cost of suffering, isolation or compulsive repetition. It often reveals a conflict between the need for connection and the fear of it, between the search for intensity and the impossibility of allowing oneself to be emotionally reached.

Contemporary psychoanalytic authors such as Vincent Estellon, Joyce McDougall, Patrick Carnes, Aviel Goodman, Shere Hite, Martin Kafka or Laurent Karila have explored the archaic roots of these behaviours. Estellon describes a defensive use of sexuality: a way of warding off the terror of loving and being loved by replacing emotional intimacy with a repetitive, often affect‑less sexual scene. McDougall speaks of “theatres of the body”, where sexuality becomes a language, sometimes the only one, through which unspoken distress finds expression.

In clinical practice, I meet patients for whom compulsive sexuality is less about pleasure than about filling a void. One man, Corentin, moves from partner to partner with a single obsession: making the other climax. Yet the moment emotional closeness becomes possible, he withdraws. What matters is not the encounter but the effect he produces. The other’s orgasm becomes a fragile proof of existence, while intimacy remains too threatening to risk.

Another patient, Tony, grew up with a father who was absent, silent, unable to embody a figure of transmission. His compulsive sexuality, driven by performance and conquest, seems to respond to a deep anxiety of inadequacy. He tries to prove he is a man, again and again, but no act is ever enough to fill the void left by the father’s absence. The sexual scene becomes a place where he attempts to repair an old narcissistic wound.

Some patients engage in codified practices such as swinging or libertinism. Ari and Jean only attend swingers’ clubs. For them, the ritual has become the only space where the couple feels alive, yet beneath the intensity lies a difficulty in meeting each other outside performance. Garance and Éluard, married for thirty years, now only find connection in the anticipation of the next encounter with others. What was once playful becomes a crutch. One of them begins to suffer, to no longer recognise themselves. The issue is not the practice itself, but the loss of meaning, the absence of speech, and the solitude within the bond.

Other situations raise questions of power and dispossession. Anna, a brilliant academic, describes BDSM practices in which she lets herself be dominated by younger men. She finds relief in this surrender, a temporary suspension of the pressure she carries. Yet this dynamic seems to replay an ambivalence: the difficulty of inhabiting her own authority, delegated instead to the other in intimacy. Again, the practice is not pathological in itself, but its compulsive, exclusive nature - and the absence of emotional connection - calls for reflection.

Karim, meanwhile, spends large sums on prostitution. He can no longer form romantic bonds. The financial transaction becomes the only space where he feels allowed to desire. “At least there, I know what I’m worth,” he says. Payment becomes a ritual of control, a way to avoid rejection, but also a way to freeze the relationship in a reassuring asymmetry.

These forms of pleasure are not inherently pathological. They may express a singular desire, a search for self, a consensual exploration. But when they become the only mode of relating, when they bring suffering, shame, isolation or chronic dissatisfaction despite the multiplication of partners or stimuli, they signal a psychic conflict that needs to be heard. Joyce McDougall notes that many of these patients have experienced early intrusion or abandonment and have built powerful defences to survive annihilation anxiety. Martin Kafka describes hypersexuality as an impulse‑control disorder, while Patrick Carnes emphasises its addictive, compulsive dimension, often linked to early trauma.

Engaging in clinical work around these practices means offering a space where the subject can question what repeats, what escapes, what hurts. It also means recognising that sexuality, in its complexity, can be a place of connection, repair or loss, depending on how it is inscribed in the subject’s history. Therapy allows meaning to re‑emerge where repetition has taken over, speech to return where the body was speaking alone, and the possibility of a less defensive, more alive relationship to the other to unfold.
