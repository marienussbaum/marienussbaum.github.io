The term _“burnout”_ has become widespread in contemporary medical, media, and managerial discourse. It refers to a state of physical, emotional, and psychological exhaustion linked to prolonged professional or relational overload. Yet this term, originally rooted in the social sciences, remains controversial in psychoanalytic circles, where one prefers to think in terms of narcissistic collapse, the fatigue of being oneself, or crises of meaning. Beyond the word itself, what interests the clinician is the subjective experience: a depleted body, a faltering desire, a silenced voice.

In clinical practice, burnout is never uniform. It does not appear as a fixed entity but as a constellation of signs shaped by the subject’s history. It is not simply overwork or chronic stress, but a breaking point in one’s relationship to oneself, to others, to the world of work, and sometimes to the ideal that once structured the subject.

Julien, a 42‑year‑old executive, describes his daily life as an endless race. In session he says, “I tick all the boxes, but I don’t feel anything anymore. Even my son, I look at him as if he were someone else’s child.” His words reveal a profound loss of affect, a disconnection between everyday actions and psychic investment. The subject is present, he functions, but he no longer experiences himself from within.

Claire, a nurse, seeks help after a long medical leave. She recounts, “I gave everything. Then one morning, I couldn’t get out of bed. As if my body decided to say no for me.” This sudden withdrawal of the body, this somatic symptom, often reflects a conflict between the ego ideal - the drive to embody a perfect, available, devoted image - and the subject’s real limits. Burnout then emerges as a collapse of narcissism, a fall of the self‑image one had constructed.

From a psychoanalytic perspective, burnout can be understood as a crisis of primary narcissism which is that early foundation on which the subject relies to feel existing, recognized, lovable. When external gratifications crumble (professional failures, conflicts, feelings of uselessness), the subject is confronted with an inner void, a hollow that activity had previously managed to fill.

Repetition is also often at play. Some patients, driven by unconscious logic, reproduce scenarios of abandonment, sacrifice, or self‑effacement rooted in their childhood history. They exhaust themselves trying to satisfy an Other (hierarchical, familial, or imaginary) at the expense of their own integrity.

Marc, a young teacher, describes a tyrannical relationship to his work: “I can’t say no. I have to be there for my students, just like my mother was always available for my father, even when she was unwell.” His family history surfaces in his speech. The symptom speaks not only of work, but of an older scenario in which the Other’s desire takes precedence over one’s own.

Therapeutic work aims to reintroduce speech where the body has begun to cry out. It involves helping the subject reclaim their psychic space, their rhythm, their limits. Psychoanalysis does not offer a standardized protocol but a framework in which the symptom can be heard as a message, not to be eradicated, but to be worked through.

The singularity of each burnout invites us to avoid rigid diagnoses and instead listen to what this collapse of body and psyche reveals about the subject, their relationship to desire, to ideals, and to their own history.
