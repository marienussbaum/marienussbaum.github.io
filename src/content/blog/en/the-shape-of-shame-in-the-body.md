Some experiences never appear as emotions. Shame arrives differently. It settles into the way a person stands, into the habit of breathing a little less deeply, a little less fully, a little less close to the world. Shame is not a confession; it is a posture. It is not a word; it is a memory. It is not simply an emotion. At times, it reflects what psychoanalyst André Green described as a narcissistic vulnerability, those fragile places where a person comes to doubt their worth, their inner continuity, or even their right to exist without diminishing themselves.

In my clinical work, I often notice that shame is not what people talk about. It is what they move around. It lives in sentences that suddenly stop, in memories that blur just when they begin to come into focus, in stories that withdraw at the very moment they approach a deeper truth.

Shame is rarely an isolated feeling. It is a way of being in the world, a way of questioning one's right to take up space or to be oneself. Boris Cyrulnik describes it as an emotional imprint, a trace that settles into gestures long before it settles into words. Donald Winnicott approached a similar reality through the concept of the “false self,” when adaptation to the environment becomes so necessary that a person gradually loses contact with their authentic experience. Serge Tisseron, in turn, writes about silent inheritances, invisible loyalties that shape intimate life without ever openly revealing themselves.

There is, for example, a woman who comes to therapy with an elegance that feels almost too perfect, as though every gesture must prove something. She speaks softly, with a restraint that resembles a quiet loyalty to a story that existed before her. She says she does not know how to apologize. She does not know how to find her place among others. She does not know how to exist without feeling as though she is somehow in the way.

She never says, “I am ashamed,” yet her entire body seems to speak those words.

In the therapy room, it becomes clear that her shame is not a flaw. It is a trace. The imprint of a childhood in which she had to be impeccable in order not to disturb, disappoint, or take up too much space. As Serge Tisseron suggests, certain forms of shame are not only emotions; they are transmissions. The work of therapy is not to eliminate shame itself, but to create the possibility of existing without constantly punishing oneself.

Within the therapeutic relationship, it sometimes becomes possible to recognize that what felt for years like a truth about oneself was also a way of staying safe. When shame can be acknowledged without judgment, it often begins to loosen its grip.

There is also the man who speaks with confidence, yet something beneath that confidence seems unsteady. He says he does not understand why he sometimes becomes angry, why he withdraws from others, or why it feels so difficult to admit when he is wrong. For much of his life, he has learned to locate his value through achievement, control, and relentless self-demand.

His shame is not immediately visible. It hides behind a constant pursuit of perfection. Over time, the therapeutic work reveals that this perfectionism is not simply a strength. It is also the mark of a personal history in which mistakes were equated with failure and vulnerability felt dangerous. What emerges in therapy is the possibility of recognizing oneself without judgment, of existing without constantly having to prove one's worth.

Shame is never merely a feeling. It is a way of protecting oneself, a way of avoiding being seen. It can be gentle, almost tender, like a form of modesty that offers protection. It can also be harsh, even painful, creating a distance that isolates.

Very often, shame is inherited. It is passed through gestures, family silences, and invisible loyalties that existed long before us.

It whispers:

“I am not sure I have the right to be here.”

“I am not sure I have the right to be myself.”

When shame begins to be shared rather than carried alone, something in the body softens. The gaze becomes a little less severe. Movements become a little less restrained. What once seemed like an undeniable truth about oneself gradually reveals itself as a story that can be told differently.

What frees us from shame is not simply naming it. It is learning to look at it without punishment. It is offering it a space where it can exist without being condemned. It is allowing it to become a story, a memory, an experience that no longer wounds in the same way.

Shame is not an enemy. It is often the trace of an old loyalty. When that knot begins to loosen, something opens. A person no longer needs to hide in order to exist and can gradually discover a freer way of inhabiting their life.
